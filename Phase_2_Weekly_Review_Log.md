#### Concept of the Week: Selling on Good News

Both CEG and MA reported strong earnings and sold off immediately. This is one of the most disorienting things a new investor encounters.

**What's happening:** Stock prices reflect *expectations*, not just results. When a company beats earnings but the stock falls, it typically means the price had already incorporated expectations *higher* than what was delivered. Investors who bought the rumor sell the news.

**The pattern:** Markets often "buy the rumor, sell the news." Prices rise in anticipation of an event, then fall when it happens — even if the event is positive.

**Why it matters here:** Neither CEG nor MA thesis broke. Both beat estimates. The sell-off is a signal about valuation and expectations, not about business quality. Per our thesis-break criteria, a quarterly beat followed by a sell-off absolutely does not constitute a thesis break.

**Rule of thumb:** Always ask "did the *business* underperform, or did the *stock price* underperform *expectations*?" Those are two different questions.

#### Concept of the Week: The Multiple Re-Rating

MU's move from $751 to $965 in a single week isn't primarily about earnings changing — it's about how the market *values* those earnings changing permanently.

**The concept:** A "re-rating" happens when investors decide a company deserves a fundamentally different price-to-earnings multiple, usually because the business's risk profile or growth trajectory has structurally shifted. Micron has historically been valued as a *cyclical* company — boom-bust memory pricing meant the market only paid 8–15x earnings through the cycle. The emerging argument is that AI memory demand (specifically HBM) is *secular* — durable, pricing-power-driven, and demand-constrained for years. If that's true, Micron deserves a growth multiple (30–50x+), not a cyclical one.

**Why this is the key question for MU:** The price target upgrades from Susquehanna ($1,750) and UBS ($1,625) represent analysts making that re-rating bet explicitly. They're saying: "This isn't a cyclical chip company anymore — value it like a secular AI infrastructure company."

**The risk:** Re-ratings can reverse violently. If memory pricing softens, if hyperscaler capex cools, or if Samsung/SK Hynix flood HBM supply, the market will re-apply the cyclical multiple and the stock could give back most of this gain in a similar time frame.

#### Concept of the Week: A "Perfect" Earnings Report Is Priced Before It Prints

MU is the cleanest case study this experiment will ever produce. Blowout numbers, raised guide, thesis vindicated — and the stock peaked the *next day* and fell.

**The idea:** Stock prices reflect expectations. Going into the print, analysts had roughly tripled price targets and the stock sat at an all-time high. The good news wasn't merely expected — it was *over*-expected and already in the price. When results only *meet* a perfect bar, there's no marginal buyer left to push the stock higher, and it falls under its own weight (here, a quarter-end sector rotation supplied the catalyst and the exit).

**Why it matters:** This braids together three concepts already in the ledger — **multiple re-rating** (W3), **priced-in / sell-the-news** (W2), and **priced for perfection** (W6) — into a single transferable rule: *the harder a stock has run into an event, the less a good outcome helps it.* The asymmetry is the point. Into an over-extended setup, a great report is the *base case* (limited upside) while any blemish is a downside surprise (large downside). Your edge as a holder is not predicting the one-day reaction — it's knowing the thesis was confirmed and not flinching when the froth comes off.

**Rule of thumb:** Before an earnings event, ask "how much of a perfect outcome is already in this price?" The more a stock has rallied into the print, the more a beat is *required* rather than *rewarded* — and the more a hold-and-don't-flinch posture (not a trade) is the right response to the post-report dip.

#### Concept of the Week: Absolute and Relative Performance Can Move in Opposite Directions

Last week's lesson was "a shrinking lead isn't always your problem" — a caution against reading a compressing gap as portfolio weakness. This week is the mirror-image and more uncomfortable version: the portfolio's *absolute* return fell almost 4 points (from +8.65% to +4.76%), while its *relative* standing held or improved (lead over SPY compressed only slightly; lead over QQQ widened).

**The idea:** Absolute return answers "did I make money this week?" (No — the book lost ~3.6% of its value.) Relative return answers "did I do better than the alternative?" (Yes — the market fell harder.) These are different questions with different answers, and in a broad-selloff week they routinely diverge. A diversified book that's designed to lose *less* on down days will, by construction, show its best *relative* numbers on its worst *absolute* weeks.

**Why it matters:** The failure mode runs both directions. Over-focusing on the preserved relative lead can lull you into ignoring that a −3.6% week is real money and real risk — here concentrated almost entirely in one overheated position (MU). Over-focusing on the ugly absolute number can make you flinch on a thesis that hasn't broken. The discipline established since Week 1 — separate thesis from price, separate mechanism from headline — extends here: separate *absolute* from *relative*, hold both readings at once, and let neither cancel the other. The experiment's success is defined relatively (beat the benchmarks), so the relative number is the scorecard — but the absolute number is the risk you're actually carrying.

**Rule of thumb:** On any big up or down week, read *both* numbers before drawing a conclusion. "Beat the benchmark" and "lost money" can both be true in the same week, and a well-built portfolio will produce exactly that combination on its worst tapes.

#### Concept of the Week: If You Can Name the Suppressant, You Have Named the Catalyst

Last week's entry closed with this sentence: *"the honest position on CEG is now that there is no identified catalyst that reprices it."* Seven days later CEG had its best week since the trough and its best close since May 30. This entry is about why that sentence was wrong, because the error is more useful than the outcome.

The **diagnosis** in W13 was, as far as I can tell, correct and should be kept. It established that what was holding CEG down was not uncertainty about performance — the Q2 audit resolved that and changed nothing — but a set of structural and valuational facts: the Calpine share overhang from W8, PJM and ERCOT regulatory uncertainty, and a multiple that the market marked down in the June rate selloff and never marked back up. An earnings report cannot address any of those. That analysis holds up perfectly.

The **conclusion drawn from it did not**. "No identified catalyst remains" only follows if "catalyst" means *a scheduled company event that resolves uncertainty* — earnings, an FERC ruling, a PPA signing. Under that definition, correctly diagnosing the suppressant as the discount rate does produce "no catalyst," because the discount rate is not on Constellation's calendar. But a suppressant and a catalyst are the same variable read in opposite directions. **If the thing holding a price down is the rate environment, then the rate environment changing is the catalyst.** Naming the mechanism and then declaring that no catalyst exists is self-contradictory, and this week a CPI print made that concrete.

**The rule:** once you have diagnosed why a price is stuck, invert the diagnosis to find what would unstick it, and write that down instead of writing "nothing." *"The catalyst is a change in rate expectations, which isn't on the company's calendar and can't be timed"* is a far more useful sentence than *"no catalyst remains."* The first tells you what to watch and correctly sets the expectation that you are waiting on the macro rather than on management. The second tells you to give up — and the practical cost is real, because acting on the W13 conclusion would have meant reconsidering the position at what has so far been the exact low of the pessimism.

**Two honest caveats, because this could easily be over-read.** First, one week is not resolution. CEG is still the only underwater position at -10.19% total, six confirmed business wins across fourteen weeks have still produced a negative return, and the core W13 observation — that being right about a business and getting paid nothing for it is exactly the failure mode this experiment was built to detect — stands untouched. Second, the *direction* of this week's move was luck. The lesson is about how the question was framed, not about who turned out to be right. #### Concept of the Week: Adjusted vs. Unadjusted Closes

A small, practical one this week, surfaced by a discrepancy that looked at first like an error.

Pulling this week's prices, stockanalysis.com reported CEG's **August 14 close as $282.07**. The W14 entry logged **$282.50**. A logged historical price had apparently changed after the fact, which is exactly the sort of thing that should stop a check-in cold. The gap is **$0.43 — precisely the $0.4265 dividend that went ex on August 18.**

Price-history providers routinely publish **adjusted** closes: once a stock goes ex-dividend, every prior close in the series is reduced by the dividend amount. The reason is sound. A stock that pays $0.43 mechanically opens about $0.43 lower on the ex-date without anything having happened to the business, so an unadjusted chart shows a fake gap down four times a year. Adjusting the history removes those artifacts, which is what you want if you are measuring **total return** from a price series alone.

It is not what this tracker wants. This log deliberately keeps **price return and dividends in separate columns** — columns C–U track price, columns V–Z add dividends back — specifically so we can see which is which. Feeding it adjusted closes would subtract the dividend from the price column and then add it back in the dividend column, understating price return and telling us nothing. So: **this experiment needs unadjusted closes, and W14's $282.50 stands.**

**The rule:** when a previously logged historical close silently changes, check the size of the change against the dividend before assuming a data error. If it matches, you are looking at an adjusted series, not a mistake — and the question to ask is which of the two your measurement actually needs. There is a related trap worth flagging for later: stock splits get the same treatment, at much larger magnitude. If any position in this book ever splits, every entry price and share count in the reference block has to be restated deliberately rather than discovered by surprise.

### What the quarter proves — and what it does not

**Proves (or at least supports):**

1. Equal-weight sizing removed the Phase 1 failure mode. No name is load-bearing.
2. Thematic breadth translated into factor breadth better than Week 4 feared — four positions each contributed $15–25 of gain by Week 14, with different drivers.

**Does not prove:**

1. **Three months is not a sample.** Fourteen weekly observations of one portfolio in one macro regime cannot distinguish skill from luck, and nothing in this review should be read as evidence of durable edge.
2. **The regime flattered the book.** A rate-relief rally in the final weeks lifted the two long-duration positions simultaneously — the same shared factor that hurt in June. The portfolio did not become better diversified; it got the macro it was already positioned for.
3. **MU's alpha is one call, not a repeatable process.** The honest question for month six is whether MU's +20.61pp was insight or a memory-cycle lottery ticket that happened to hit.
4. **The picks were Claude's, not mine.** Phase 2's positions were suggested, not independently sourced. Whatever the six-month verdict is, it scores a recommendation process — worth remembering before generalizing to my own decisions.

### Decisions for months 4–6

**Hold all five. No reallocation, no trimming, no additions.** Rationale: no thesis-break criterion has triggered on any position, the experiment's value comes from a clean six-month observation, and trading against a favorable three-month reading would destroy the very evidence being gathered.

Specific items to carry forward:

- **CEG stays the decisive position.** It is the only failure and the richest source of learning. The question for months 4–6 is not "will it recover" but "does the eight-point idiosyncratic gap close, and if so on what." A recovery on rate relief alone would confirm that the market never disputed the business — only its multiple.
- **MU is the position to watch for risk, not return.** The supply-cycle question flagged in Week 13 (Citi's 2027 memory-price peak, SK Hynix's $38B build) is unresolved and was deferred, not answered, by Week 14's rally. If it resolves badly, the largest single source of alpha reverses.
- **Re-run this sector attribution at six months.** The theme-selection versus stock-selection split is the single most informative number this experiment produces, and one reading is not a trend.
- **Watch whether the Week 14 dispersion holds.** Four positions contributing near-evenly is the hypothesis working. If MU pulls away again, the book is back to a Phase 1 shape and the conclusion changes.

### What would change the verdict

- Any position triggering a documented thesis-break criterion (see `thesis-criteria.md`).
- The stock-selection alpha going negative at six months — i.e. the five stocks underperforming the five sector ETFs. That would mean the themes were right and the picks were not, and the honest conclusion would be to index the themes.
- MU's alpha reversing while the others fail to widen, which would reveal the edge as a single lucky call.
- The portfolio's lead over SPY compressing toward zero on the portfolio's own weakness rather than the benchmark's strength (the Week 9 test, applied at quarter scale).

| W11 | Some Theses Only Reprice on the Scoreboard | Contract awards and announcements are inputs to a thesis; earnings are the audit of it. A stock can ignore weeks of confirming news and then reprice violently when the report lands — if a position keeps generating good news with no price response, the answer is patience through the next earnings date, not doubt about the thesis |
| W12 | A Repricing Is an Event, Not a New Regime | An earnings audit settles the accumulated gap between news and price once — it does not convert the stock into one that trades on news flow. LMT gained 10.54% in a session on Q2, then finished the very next week +0.02% despite the largest Patriot missile award in history. Budget for one move per audit, not a changed regime |
| W13 | A Clean Audit Doesn't Guarantee a Repricing | An earnings audit is necessary for a stuck stock to reprice, not sufficient. CEG delivered a 9.5% EPS beat, raised guidance, 920MW of new nuclear PPAs and a 20%-growth path — and gained 2.72% in a week the Nasdaq did 5.09%. Before betting on earnings to unstick a price, name what you think the market is discounting and check whether a quarterly report can answer it: information gaps close on reports, structural and valuation gaps don't |
| W14 | If You Can Name the Suppressant, You Have Named the Catalyst | W13 correctly diagnosed CEG's problem as a de-rated multiple and rate sensitivity rather than performance uncertainty — then concluded "no identified catalyst remains." One week later a soft CPI print gave CEG its best week since the trough. A suppressant and a catalyst are the same variable read in opposite directions: if rates are what's holding a price down, a change in rates is the catalyst. Invert the diagnosis and write down what would unstick it, rather than writing "nothing" |
| W15 | Adjusted vs. Unadjusted Closes | stockanalysis.com reported CEG's Aug 14 close as $282.07 against the $282.50 logged in W14 — a gap of exactly the $0.4265 Aug 18 ex-dividend. Price providers retroactively subtract dividends from prior closes so total-return charts don't show a false gap down on the ex-date. This tracker separates price return from dividends by design, so it needs unadjusted closes; the adjusted figure would double-count. When a logged historical close changes, check the change against the dividend before assuming a data error — and expect the same effect at much larger magnitude on any stock split |
| W17 | Appropriation Risk Is Separate From Demand Risk | A government contract is an authorization to be paid money Congress has not yet provided. On September 1 a continuing resolution froze the Pentagon at $838.5B against a $1.5T request with no new program starts, and LMT fell 6.84% in a week with no bad company news. Backlog converts on the appropriations calendar, not the award date. For any holding whose revenue comes from government budgets, track that calendar the way you track earnings |
| W18 | The Rate Factor Reaches Everything (amendment to Lesson 5) | Lesson 5 (W4) established that MU and CEG shared a hidden rate factor and named LLY the book's one genuine diversifier, on low beta and healthcare-specific drivers. The five themes are uncorrelated to each other on company news and correlated to the discount rate. The exception named in Lesson 5 does not survive a large enough shock |
