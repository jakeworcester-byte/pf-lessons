# Phase 2 — Lessons Ledger

Append-only. One entry per lesson. Only what's durable and transferable.

---

## Carried from Phase 1

**1. Sizing matters more than picking.** Phase 1's smallest allocation (AMD at 15%) was the biggest winner; the largest (NVDA at 25%) was the smallest gainer. Don't let conviction drive oversized positions — equal-weight until you have evidence of edge.

**2. Oversized speculation hurts.** IONQ at 20% (pre-revenue, speculative) lost 31% and dragged total return. Keep speculative positions small or eliminate them entirely. Phase 2 has no pre-revenue names as a direct result of this lesson.

**3. One stock can mask a bad portfolio.** Phase 1 returned +16.5%, but stripping out AMD, the rest returned ~3%. Broad thematic conviction was not validated — one hot sector carried everything.

## Phase 2 Lessons

**4. Check the earnings calendar before entry.** *(Week 1 — May 15, 2026)* CEG reported Q1 earnings on May 11, the same day we bought. We walked into a same-day earnings position with zero ability to react to the market's interpretation. The stock beat and raised guidance but sold off on nuclear restart delays. Entry price was avoidably worse. Add earnings calendar check to the pre-flight checklist for any future entry.

**5. Different sectors are not the same as different risk factors.** *(Week 4 — June 5, 2026)* The portfolio was built around five themes treated as uncorrelated. On June 5, a blowout jobs report spiked rate-hike fears and MU (AI memory) and CEG (nuclear power) — supposedly unrelated themes — both fell hard on the *same* trigger. The reason: both are valued on long-dated future cash flows (MU's secular re-rating, CEG's 20-year contracts), making both long-duration assets that sell off when rates rise. Meanwhile LLY, with low beta (~0.31) and healthcare-specific drivers, hit an all-time high the same day — the only genuine diversifier in the book. The lesson: when assessing whether positions diversify each other, ask "what single macro event would make both fall at once?" not "are these in different industries?" Two themes that share a hidden factor (here, rates) are less diversified than the theme labels suggest. This is the first Phase 2 lesson about portfolio *design* rather than any individual position — and it's a partial early answer to the core hypothesis: thematic breadth on paper isn't the same as factor breadth in practice.

> **Amendment (Week 18, September 11, 2026).** This entry named LLY the book's one genuine diversifier on the evidence of a single day. In W18, the first week of Phase 2 in which all five positions fell, core CPI at +0.3% against +0.2% expected moved September FOMC hike odds to roughly 86 to 90% and the 10-year to about 4.975%, and LLY fell 2.93% alongside MU, CEG, LMT and MA while the book dropped about three and a half times as hard as SPY.
> The mechanism in this lesson holds and the exception does not: a large enough rate shock reaches every position, so treat the five themes as uncorrelated on company news and correlated on the discount rate.

**6. A confirmed thesis is not the same as price movement.** *(Week 5 — June 12, 2026)* CEG's FERC waiver — the exact regulatory catalyst we'd flagged as the binary decision point for the Three Mile Island restart — was *granted*, a clear thesis-positive resolution. The stock barely moved and stayed ~19% underwater. The catalyst confirmed the business case; the price was simultaneously being held down by separate forces (the June 1 dilutive secondary below cost basis, plus broad rate/utility-valuation overhang). The lesson: when a catalyst resolves, score the *thesis* first ("did the business case improve?") and the *price* separately ("why did or didn't it follow?"). Conflating the two is how investors talk themselves out of good positions at the bottom — and conversely, a resolved catalyst in your favor is a reason for *more* conviction, regardless of the day's price action. This is the operational complement to the thesis-break discipline: thesis-tracking and price-tracking are two different instruments.

**7. Beta cuts both ways — "defensive" is relative to a specific risk factor.** *(Week 6 — June 19, 2026)* In Week 4, LLY's low beta made it the hero, rising while the market sold off. In Week 6, LMT fell ~4% *on a market-up day* because the rally's cause — a US-Iran peace agreement — was precisely the thing that softens near-term defense sentiment. LMT is low-beta to the broad market but high-beta to geopolitical conflict; it is effectively *long conflict*. The lesson: a position's "defensive" character is never universal — it's always defensive (or offensive) relative to *which* risk factor you measure against. The same macro trigger (geopolitics) can be a tailwind for one holding and a headwind for another in the same book. That offsetting sensitivity — LMT's conflict exposure against MU/CEG's rate exposure — is genuine diversification working as designed, and it's the mirror image of Lesson 5: just as two "different" sectors can share a hidden factor, one "defensive" name can carry a hidden directional bet. Before calling anything defensive, finish the sentence: defensive *against what?*

**8. A "perfect" earnings report is priced before it prints — the harder a stock runs into an event, the less a good outcome helps it.** *(Week 7 — June 26, 2026)* MU reported a genuine blowout on June 24 (EPS $25.11 vs. $20.86 est., revenue $41.46B, a $50B Q4 guide), fully validating the "down the stack" memory thesis. The stock jumped ~20% after-hours, hit an all-time high of $1,255 the next day — and then fell -6.69% on June 26, giving back a slice of froth in a quarter-end tech rotation. The reason: going into the print, analysts had roughly tripled price targets and the stock was already at an ATH, so a perfect outcome was the *base case* already in the price, not a positive surprise. When results merely *meet* a perfect bar, there's no marginal buyer left and the stock can fall under its own weight. This braids together three earlier concepts — re-rating (Lesson context, W3), priced-in/sell-the-news (W2), and priced for perfection (W6) — into one transferable rule: into an over-extended setup, a great report is required rather than rewarded, the payoff is asymmetric (limited upside, real downside on any blemish), and the correct response to the post-report dip is to hold and not flinch, because the *thesis* got stronger even as the *price* pulled back. The investor's edge isn't predicting the one-day reaction — it's separating "was the business case confirmed?" (yes) from "did the stock go up that day?" (no), which is the direct operational sibling of Lesson 6 (confirmed thesis ≠ price movement). Before any earnings event you hold into, ask: *how much of a perfect outcome is already in this price?*

**9. Diversification proves itself on the leader's bad day — but check what's actually doing the proving.** *(Weeks 7–8 — June 26 & July 2, 2026)* Through Week 7, MU had carried roughly 90% of the portfolio's cumulative gains — real concentration risk, echoing the Phase 1 pattern. Over the next two weeks, MU stumbled twice: the post-earnings rotation on June 26, then a sharper pullback in Week 8 after Michael Burry disclosed a short position. Both weeks, LLY, LMT, and MA picked up the slack, and the portfolio kept outperforming both benchmarks even as its largest position bled. That's the textbook case for holding a diversified book: it should earn its keep precisely when the star performer cools off, not only when everything rises together. But the mechanism deserves to be named precisely rather than credited to "diversification" in the abstract. LLY/LMT/MA's strength across both weeks was substantially a *sector-rotation* trade — money leaving AI/tech and landing in healthcare, defense, and payments — not five independently-uncorrelated theses each firing on separate logic. That is a real and legitimate form of diversification (rotation dynamics are themselves a risk factor worth being diversified against), but it is a narrower claim than "these themes don't share risk factors," and it doesn't extend to every position in the book. CEG's continued weakness in Week 8 — driven by a Calpine lock-up share overhang and a Citi price-target cut, not by the tech rotation — is idiosyncratic, company-specific risk that the other four positions aren't diversifying at all. The portfolio's headline outperformance can and does coexist with one position's thesis sitting under real, uncovered pressure. The lesson: when a diversified book "proves itself" on a bad week for the leader, identify the actual mechanism (sector rotation, genuinely uncorrelated fundamentals, or something else) before crediting diversification broadly — and don't let portfolio-level outperformance paper over a single position that still needs its own scrutiny.

**10. A shrinking lead over the benchmark isn't always the portfolio's problem.** *(Week 9 — July 9, 2026)* For three straight weeks, the portfolio's outperformance margin over SPY and QQQ compressed (W7: +11.11pp/+10.66pp → W8: +8.43pp/+9.26pp → W9: +6.98pp/+7.25pp), which on its face looks like fading momentum. But the portfolio's own total return barely moved over that span (10.00% at its W6 peak → 8.65% in W9, a slow one-point-a-week drift), while SPY and QQQ both had unusually strong weeks — both hitting fresh all-time highs in W9 alone, partly on Broadcom's Apple deal lifting chip sentiment broadly. The relative-performance number is a function of *both* sides; a narrowing gap can come from the portfolio slipping, the benchmark surging, or some mix of both, and it's easy to default to blaming the portfolio when in fact the benchmark side did nearly all the moving. The lesson: before treating a shrinking lead as underperformance, check which side of the comparison actually changed. This is the mirror-image caution to the outperformance discipline established since Week 1 — just as portfolio outperformance shouldn't be over-credited without checking the mechanism (Lesson 9), portfolio "underperformance" (relative to a prior week's bigger lead) shouldn't be assumed without checking whether the benchmark simply had a hot stretch.

**11. Absolute and relative performance can move in opposite directions — on a stress week, a well-built book shows its best relative numbers on its worst absolute weeks.** *(Week 10 — July 17, 2026)* Week 10 was the portfolio's worst absolute week of Phase 2: total return fell nearly four points (from +8.65% to +4.76%) as MU cratered -14.4% in a broad AI/momentum unwind (SK Hynix's US listing, a global chip selloff, the Nikkei down ~5%), extending MU's slide to roughly -25% off its late-June ATH. And yet the portfolio's *relative* standing held or improved: the lead over SPY compressed only slightly (to +4.22pp) and the lead over QQQ actually *widened* (+7.25pp → +7.27pp), because the selloff hit the benchmarks — QQQ especially, which fell back below its Phase-2 baseline — harder than it hit the diversified book. This is the direct extension of Lesson 10, but sharper and in the opposite direction: Lesson 10 warned that a *shrinking lead* isn't automatically portfolio weakness (the benchmark may just be hot); Lesson 11 is the mirror — a *bad absolute week* isn't automatically relative failure (the benchmark may be worse). Absolute return answers "did I make money?" (no — the book lost ~3.6% of its value); relative return answers "did I beat the alternative?" (yes — the market fell harder). A portfolio built to lose *less* on down days will, by construction, produce its best relative readings precisely on its ugliest absolute tapes, because that's the day the diversification is supposed to pay off. The failure mode runs both ways: over-focusing on the preserved relative lead lulls you into ignoring that a -3.6% week is real risk concentrated in one overheated name (MU); over-focusing on the ugly absolute number makes you flinch on a thesis that hasn't broken. The discipline — the same "hold two readings at once" muscle behind Lessons 5, 6, 8, 9, and 10 (thesis vs. price, mechanism vs. headline, benchmark-side vs. portfolio-side) — extends here to absolute vs. relative. The experiment is scored *relatively* (beat SPY and QQQ over six months), so the relative number is the scorecard; but the absolute number is the risk actually being carried, and both must be read before drawing any conclusion about a given week.

**12. Some theses only reprice on the scoreboard — contract awards are inputs, earnings are the audit.** *(Week 11 — July 24, 2026)* For three consecutive entries this log recorded the same observation about LMT: enormous, thesis-confirming contract news arriving and the stock refusing to respond. Week 7 brought a $35B THAAD interceptor award (a 4x production increase); Week 9 brought the Ultra Maritime acquisition plus NATO and ATACMS deals, and the stock *fell* on a Wells Fargo sector-wide price-target cut; Week 10 brought a $10.53B twelve-year USSOCOM award, a $1.6B Navy order, a $439M ATACMS action and more, and the stock fell 1.83%. Each week the entry concluded, correctly, that price was moving on defense-sector sentiment rather than on backlog, and that sector sentiment does not constitute a thesis break. Then Lockheed reported Q2 on July 23 — beating and raising its 2026 forecast, with the Pentagon's weapons-restocking cycle explicitly cited — and the stock gained **10.54% in a single session and 14.51% on the week**, with price targets jumping to $600–700 (Baird, Morgan Stanley, RBC) inside of a day. Nothing about the business changed between July 22 and July 23. What changed is that the backlog stopped being a claim in a press release and became a number in an audited income statement with management guidance attached to it. Markets systematically discount announcement-flow — awards are hard to size, easy to double-count, frequently already embedded in guidance, and sometimes never convert — and wait for the earnings report to score them. The lesson is the constructive mirror of Lessons 6 and 8: Lesson 6 established that a confirmed catalyst doesn't guarantee price movement, and Lesson 8 that a *perfect* report is priced before it prints. The synthesis is that when good news is genuinely *not* priced in — when a stock has spent weeks visibly ignoring its own catalysts — the earnings report becomes a forcing function, and the repricing can be violent in your favor. The three weeks of frustration were the setup for the week of repricing. Operationally: if a position keeps generating confirming news with no price response, the correct posture is patience through the next earnings date, not a re-examination of the thesis. And this applies forward immediately — **CEG is in week eleven of exactly this pattern** (Walmart PPA, FERC waiver granted, PJM capacity auction cleared, NY license renewals, the Blue Energy SMR stake, a Morgan Stanley target of $366 against a ~$274 price), with a price that has only just begun to follow. Its Q2 report in early August is the single highest-signal event remaining in the book.

**13. A repricing is an event, not a new regime — an earnings audit settles the backlog once; it does not convert the stock into one that trades on news flow.** *(Week 12 — July 31, 2026)* Lesson 12 was written on July 24 and tested one week later, about as fast as a lesson can be tested. Its prediction was that LMT's ignored contract awards would reprice when earnings audited them — correct, +10.54% in a session. The tempting *extrapolation* was that the market had now started paying attention to Lockheed's backlog. That extrapolation was wrong, and the following week made the point unmissable: on July 29 the Department of War awarded Lockheed **$58.62B for multiyear PAC-3 MSE production, the largest Patriot missile contract in history** (Reuters), followed by a $107.29M Navy order and a Barron's piece headlined "Missiles Are Hot. That's Good for Lockheed Martin Stock." The stock finished the week **+0.02%**. The earnings report had not changed how the market values LMT. It had corrected one specific, accumulated gap between announced business and reflected price — a discrete settlement of an old debt — and then handed the stock straight back to defense-sector sentiment, which is exactly where it had been trading for the prior three weeks. The distinction matters because the two readings generate opposite expectations: "the market is now watching" predicts a strong week on a record award; "the market cleared its backlog once" predicts nothing in particular, which is what happened. This is the same discipline as Lessons 9 and 10 applied to a different axis — identify the *mechanism* before generalizing from the outcome — and it adds a specific caution to Lesson 12: the patience-through-earnings posture is right, but its payoff is a single re-rating, not a change in how the position behaves afterward. Applied forward to **CEG**, whose Q2 report (~Aug 6) is the event Lesson 12 pointed at: if the repricing comes, budget for one move and then a return to the same pattern of ignored news flow — not a stock that suddenly responds to nuclear-power headlines. That is a materially different expectation than "CEG's news flow will start working," and it should shape how the position is judged through September rather than being re-litigated week to week.

**14. When a stock's price is being set by interest rates instead of its own results, stop expecting company news to move it, and stop changing its thesis rating when the macro moves.** *(Weeks 13 to 15, August 7 to August 21, 2026. This entry replaces the former Lessons 14, 15 and 16, which were three consecutive weekly attempts at one idea. Merged August 24, 2026 at Jake's direction; the originals are preserved in the Week 13, 14 and 15 log entries.)*

**What happened, in order.** W13: CEG posted a clean Q2 (EPS $2.55 vs $2.34, guidance raised, 920MW of new nuclear PPAs) and gained 2.72%, behind QQQ, in a week that also delivered a rate-relief rally. I had predicted earnings would unstick it. W14: soft CPI collapsed rate expectations; CEG rose 4.67% on zero company news, and I upgraded its thesis status to "Pressured, improving." W15: yields snapped back; CEG fell 3.41% on zero company news, and I reversed the upgrade. Fifteen weeks, six confirmed business wins, and a -13.11% total return.

**Three rules that follow.**

1. Before betting on an earnings report to reprice a stuck stock, write down what you think the market is discounting. An earnings report answers doubt about *performance*. It cannot answer doubt about share supply, regulation, or what multiple the business deserves. If you can't name the thing, you don't have a catalyst, you have a hope.
2. If the thing holding a price down is macro, say that, and say you can't time it. "No catalyst remains" is wrong. So is naming a date.
3. Never move a thesis status on a macro-driven price move, in either direction. Company facts move the status. Rates move the price note.

**What this cost:** two weeks of thesis-status churn that tracked the ten-year yield instead of tracking Constellation.

**What to carry to the six-month review:** the CEG theme was right, the business executed, and the multiple ate the return. Say it that way. Don't collapse it into "AI power was a bad pick."

**15. Before treating a defense backlog as revenue, check whether Congress has appropriated the money.** *(Week 17 — September 4, 2026)*

Congress passed a continuing resolution on September 1 freezing federal spending through December 11, opening the Pentagon's fiscal 2027 at an $838.5B baseline against the $1.5T requested, and LMT fell 6.84% on the week with no bad company news, cutting its cumulative return from +11.92% to +4.27%.

A signed defense contract is an authorization to be paid money Congress has not yet provided. Under a CR no new programs start and obligations slide, so a record $230B backlog converts later than the award dates imply, and budget analysts do not expect full appropriations before spring 2027. Appropriation risk is a separate risk factor from the geopolitical demand the LMT thesis was built on, and it is the one that moved the stock.

For any holding whose revenue comes from a government budget, track the appropriations calendar the way I track the earnings calendar. Next date: December 11, 2026.
